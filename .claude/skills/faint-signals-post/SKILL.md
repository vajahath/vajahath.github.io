# Writing a Faint Signals post

The input is Vaju talking. He dictates by voice, so what arrives is
transcribed speech: unpunctuated in places, circling back on itself,
occasionally misheard by the transcriber. That is the raw material. Your job is
to turn it into a post that still sounds like the person who said it.

Read `references/voice.md` before drafting. It holds real samples of how he
actually talks. Match those, not your instinct for how a blog post should
sound.

## 1. Find the feeling first

Before writing a word, name what he was feeling. Out loud, in your own head:
is this a rant? Is he calm and thinking out loud? Amused? Frustrated with
something that wasted his time? Remembering a place he liked? Excited about
something he just figured out?

The whole post takes that register. An angry post and a travel memory should
not read the same way. If the feeling shifts partway through the dump — he
starts annoyed and ends up curious — let the post shift too. That movement is
usually the best thing in it.

If you genuinely cannot tell, ask him one short question. Don't guess.

## 2. Treat the dump as material, not an outline

Take what is useful. Drop the rest. He says so himself — he brain-dumps, and
not all of it is meant to survive.

He does not speak in the order a reader needs. Reorder freely. What you keep
is his: the observations, the opinions, the small details, the moments he
lingered on. What you drop is the throat-clearing and the repeated attempts at
the same sentence.

Length follows the material. A short dump makes a short post. Never pad.

## 3. How he sounds

- **"I", not "we".** Only use "we" if other people genuinely did the work
  alongside him.
- **Short sentences.** If a sentence needs re-reading, split it.
- **Simple English.** Plain words a reader gets on the first pass. No jargon
  unless the subject truly requires it, and then explain it in a line.
- **High level first, then deeper.** He opens with the big picture, then digs
  in — and he takes the reader down with him. Signal the descent. Don't drop
  someone straight into the detail.
- **Slightly imperfect, on purpose.** Asides. Second thoughts. A "right?"
  aimed at the reader. Admitting he isn't sure, or that he was wrong. This is
  the point — it is what makes it sound like a person.
  Not this: "uh", "um", "you know", "like I said". Speech filler is noise, not
  texture.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Name the actual thing. The real street, the real
  error message, the real amount of time it took.
- **Repetition is allowed** when he leaned on a point. He repeats himself for
  emphasis when something matters to him.

## 4. Quoting other people

He often quotes what someone said to him. Keep those close to the actual words.
Never smooth a quote into something cleaner than the person said.

If it wasn't in English, give the original line first, then his rough English
translation, and say that it is rough:

> "Sumimasen, kochira desu."
>
> Roughly: "Excuse me, it's this way." — or that's what I understood, anyway.

He often doesn't speak the language. That uncertainty stays in. Don't present a
confident translation he never made.

## 5. Titles

The title has to sound like something he'd say out loud.

- No colon subtitles. Not "The Making of X: Architecture of a Y".
- No coined, capitalised concepts.
- No headline-ese. This is not a newspaper front page, whatever the site's
  design looks like.

Plain and a little specific beats clever. "Why I stopped using X." "Three days
in a town I couldn't pronounce." "This took me four hours and it shouldn't
have."

## 6. Never write like this

Every one of these came off the old posts. They are the thing he called
cringey.

- Coined terms in bold, as if naming a product: **the Coordinators Paradox**,
  **Asynchronous Coordination**.
- Numbered lists of principles with bolded lead-ins.
- "The Problem with X" → "The Solution" section structure.
- "It's more than just a folder; it's a structural connection between ideas."
- Aphorisms presented as wisdom. "Good design is invisible."
- Marketing-register descriptions: "engineered for high-voltage clarity".
- Editorial "we" for work he did alone.
- Section headings that announce rather than say anything: "The Vision",
  "The Philosophy", "What's Next?".

If a sentence would be at home in a launch announcement, cut it.

## 7. Read it back before anything else

Show him the draft as prose. Ask what's off — the voice, the title, anything
that isn't his.

Only once he's happy with the words do you touch frontmatter, tags, homepage
slots, series wiring or the build. That is all in the `faint-signals-series`
skill. Use it; don't repeat it here.

## 8. Keep the samples growing

Every new dump is another sample of how he talks. When a post is done, append
the useful part of the raw dump to `references/voice.md` with a one-line note
on the register it was in. The file gets better the more it holds.
